Paul's Diagnosis: What He Actually Said

The March 5 call was not a typical board check-in. Paul came prepared, had read the revised financial model, and delivered a pointed assessment. The core message: this is a people and process problem, not a product problem.

The Central Indictment: "You're presenting a 12-month deferral and a 75% revenue haircut. But if your features ship faster than your sales cycle, the product pause doesn't explain the gap. You should be at a 25% haircut — a 3-month delay — not 75%."

Paul identified a pattern where multiple explanations for the revenue miss were offered — "we paused for product," "we just started selling two weeks ago," "we've been selling all along" — and none were internally consistent. His word: "alibi stories." He's not questioning the team's talent or the product's quality. He's questioning whether the CEO is managing toward revenue with the same rigor applied to product engineering.

The Four Structural Requirements

Paul distilled his feedback into four concrete asks. These aren't suggestions — they're conditions for maintaining board confidence:

  1. Consolidated weighted pipeline by product line — one place, all accounts, with budget/buyer/timing data to calculate probability-weighted revenue
  2. Common dashboard for marketing outreach — but reoriented from click-tracking to conversion-tracking; stop measuring what doesn't lead to money
  3. Product pipeline with revenue and costing — every sprint item must show revenue impact and engineering cost; John needs to own this
  4. Conversion process upgrades — shift from digital marketing to founder-led selling, personal connections, in-person meetings, and systematic capture of deal intelligence
The Meta-Message: Paul explicitly said: "I could look at this very quickly and say, hey, we're going to miss your revised downward plan too. I know it already." He's telling you the current trajectory doesn't hit even the bear case — and the only way to change that is structural process changes, not more product work.

Point 3: Product Pipeline Needs Revenue and Costing

What Paul Found

James showed the product ideas dashboard during the call — it has owner, strategic link, customer segment, problem statement, hypothesis, impact/effort scoring, sprint assignment. Paul scanned it and immediately asked: "What's missing from this dashboard?"

James answered correctly: "What revenue it could generate."

Paul's follow-up was equally pointed: the product pipeline was also heavily skewed toward Azimuth, with nothing visible for Token Custody or Risk Insurance. Whether that was a filter issue or a real prioritization gap, the optics were terrible.

Paul's Board-Meeting Observation: "I mentioned [revenue and costing] to John on the board call. When you don't look at it from a revenue perspective, that's why you're in two products that may not be the largest revenue drivers."

What the Product Pipeline Needs

Two new columns, minimum:

New Column Definition How to Calculate
Revenue Impact Estimated annual revenue this feature enables or unblocks Sum of weighted pipeline deals that require this feature × deal size. If Kraken needs audit logs to close at $360K/yr, audit logs have $360K revenue impact.
Engineering Cost Story points or person-weeks to ship Standard sprint estimation. Convert to dollar cost using loaded engineer rate.

With these two columns, every sprint planning session becomes a revenue-per-engineering-dollar optimization problem instead of a "what feels important" gut call.

The Feature Request Pipeline from Sales

Paul described a feedback loop that isn't currently working:

  1. Sales has a meeting with Kraken
  2. Kraken says "we need audit logs and SOC2 compliance reporting"
  3. That feature request should immediately appear in the product pipeline with Kraken's deal value attached
  4. John and the team debate: "Is building this worth $360K/year?"

Point 4: Conversion Process Upgrades

Paul's Core Message

This was the sharpest feedback of the call. Paul's diagnosis:

Direct Quote: "You're still in denial that you have to close sales. Common dashboard for marketing outreach isn't going to help you. You have a lot of leads. What you're afraid to do is have a dinner and meet people face to face to go ask them the three questions: How much budget do you have? When do you need to spend it? And are you the decision maker?"

The conversion problem isn't lead generation. It's converting leads into revenue. The team has named accounts, email contacts, demo links. What's missing is the final mile: asking for the money.

Paul's Three Conversion Questions

Every sales conversation should capture these three data points. If you leave a meeting without them, the meeting was incomplete:

  1. Who is the budget owner? — Named person, title, authority level
  2. What is the budget? — Dollar amount, annual or monthly
  3. What is the timeline? — When do they need to decide by?

Founder-Led Selling

Paul was unequivocal: "Matt, you're in founder-led selling. You and James have to be selling." Specifically:

  • Every account over $100K/month should have Matt and James on it
  • Consultants and contractors haven't worked for exchange sales — the founders need to be the face
  • The follow-up with Kraken was good (30-minute turnaround, transcript analysis, team email) — do this for every major account
  • But also: go beyond email. Set up in-person meetings. Attend events. Host dinners.

Reforecast Framework

Paul's math was simple and damning:

  • October board plan: $4M revenue for 2026
  • Current revised plan: ~$1M (a 75% haircut)
  • Paul's acceptable range: $3M (a 25% haircut — "3-month hiccup")

The gap between $1M and $3M is the "execution improvement" Paul is asking for. He's not saying the $4M was easy — he's saying a team that made product improvements, gained Coinbase as a customer, and has a market tailwind should not be reforecasting down 75%.

How to Build a Credible Reforecast

  1. Start with the weighted pipeline (Point 1). Sum it up. That's your floor.
  2. Add conversion from current leads. If you have 50 exchanges in your pipeline and historically close 5-10%, apply that rate.
  3. Add self-serve revenue. If light scan goes live and 1% of landing page visitors convert at $X/month, model it.
  4. Add Azimuth. Separate product line, separate forecast.
  5. Present three scenarios: Bear (weighted pipeline only), Base (pipeline + conversion), Bull (pipeline + conversion + self-serve + new product).
The Story Paul Wants to Hear: "Paul, here's our weighted pipeline: $X. Here's what we're doing to expand it. We had a 3-month product-building period. We learned from it — we now ship features in 2 weeks, not 2 months. Our revised forecast is $Y, which is [XX]% of the original plan. Here's the backup data."

Key principle from Paul: "Most top performers love stretch goals. They love the pursuit. And if they fall short, they're still much better off. I believe you have a strong team. If you don't shoot large, you're just not tapping into the potential."

Weekly Rhythm

Paul runs one-week sprints at Decasonic. He reviews deal pipelines weekly. TestMachine should match that cadence:

  • Monday: Pipeline review — what moved, what stalled, what's new
  • Wednesday: Sprint check — are we building what drives revenue?
  • Friday: Weekly summary to Paul — 3 bullets: pipeline delta, key meetings, sprint output
Paul's Parting Words: "Iterate faster with me. I'm tremendously busy, dude. I gotta work late now on my other stuff. I'm carving out time to try and get this over. Lean into it, take accountability, and I'm along here to help you guys out."

The Relationship Dynamic

Paul ended the call with a critical point that should not be missed: "I'll always poke and prod for the service of the company and being better." He's not hostile — he's frustrated because he sees potential being wasted. The team needs to lean into his expertise rather than defending against his questions.

The most productive dynamic going forward:

  • Proactive updates — don't wait for Paul to ask. Send the data before the meeting.
  • Honest gaps — "Paul, I don't know how to do this. Help me." is a valid and welcome statement.
  • Fast iteration — 24-hour turnarounds, not 2-week delays. Show responsiveness.
  • Stretch targets — present ambitious goals with clear execution plans. Fall short with dignity, not with excuses.
The Opportunity: Paul said he has "a little bit more confidence than before. At least it's trending in the right direction." The process changes in this plan are straightforward — 80 data points, a few new columns, a CRM, and a shift from marketing metrics to sales metrics. None of this is hard. It just needs to get done. Starting now.
